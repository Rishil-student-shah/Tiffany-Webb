What merges, what splits, and why.

Between her two pages and our build there are roughly thirty section ideas. Most of the redundancy is that four different sections answer the same buyer question. Grouping by question answered rather than by content type collapses thirty into twelve.

Seven merges

Merge 1 — Credentials
Professional Expertise (14) + Career Highlights (8) + Credential bar (3 stats) → one Credentials block

Why: all three answer one question — is she qualified? Split across three sections, each looks thin. Combined, fourteen expertise areas, eight roles and two hard numbers make a dense, unarguable case. Home gets the three stats only; /about gets the full block.

Merge 2 — Ways to work with her
Her "Services Available For" (14) + our "Six ways to bring her in" (6) → six cards plus a long-tail list

Why: fourteen equal-weight cards is unshoppable; six alone throws away eight bookable services. Six primary formats as cards, then a plain line — "also available for podcast interviews, webinars, staff development, strategic planning, coalition building and community consultation" — keeps the long tail discoverable for search and for organisers with unusual asks, without diluting the six.

Merge 3 — Where she works
Audiences Served + Who Tiffany Loves To Work With + our "Where she works" → one section of 11

Why: these are the same list printed three times in her material. One section, eleven entries, adding faith communities and conference organisers. Our build already got this right — it just needs the three missing entries.

Merge 4 — Partnership
SPEAK · PARTNER · STRATEGIZE · STRENGTHEN + Organizations & Partnerships → one Partnership module

Why: the four-pillar framework explains how she partners; the organisation list proves who with. Apart, the framework is an unsupported claim and the list is trivia. Together they're a case. This is also the section that sells consulting rather than speaking, which is the higher-value half of the business model.

Merge 5 — Why book her
Why Organizations Choose Tiffany (11 attributes) + Testimonials → one proof module

Why: a list of eleven adjectives about yourself is self-assessment. The same list with three named quotes underneath becomes evidence. Neither works alone — and this is exactly why the section should not ship until real testimonials exist.

Merge 6 — Media
Media Resources (10 assets) + speaker one-sheet + press kit + logos + approved bios → /media

Why: journalists and conference marketing staff need to self-serve at 11pm without emailing her. One page, everything downloadable, three bio lengths, two headshot crops. This also removes the "available upon request" friction — which in practice means "unavailable".

Merge 7 — Impact
Upcoming Events + past engagements + outcomes + partners & collaborations → /impact

Why: this is the page Tiffany asked for in the meeting, and it answers the only question a booking committee really has — has this worked before, for people like us? Full specification in panel 5.

Five splits

  • /speaking — Twenty topics at roughly ninety words each is about 1,800 words. That is a page, not a homepage module. It is also the site's entire search engine: each topic is a phrase somebody types. Homepage keeps the flagship plus four track cards and links out.
  • /about — Story, credentials, values, GambleFreeGear, the long bio. The homepage version stays at three paragraphs.
  • /impact — Events, outcomes, partners. See panel 5.
  • /media — Press kit, downloads, bios, headshots, logo usage.
  • /book — The qualified form and the client journey. This is the CRM and WhatsApp entry point, so it needs its own URL for tracking and for pasting into emails.

Optional sixth: /insights for the blog articles already written, and a decision on whether GambleFreeGear gets a page here or lives entirely at inpowerimports.com.

The event page she asked for.

She named three things in the meeting: the client journey, outcomes of events, and partners she has collaborated with. She wasn't sure they all belonged on the event page, and asked us to advise. Here is the recommendation, with one disagreement stated openly rather than resolved quietly.

Where each of her three asks belongs

Outcomes of events → /impact — yes, exactly where she said

Outcomes are the most persuasive asset a speaker can own and the site currently has none. Each past engagement gets an outcome block: audience size and type, what was covered, and what changed afterwards — screenings booked, a resolution passed, staff trained, referrals made, students reached. Plus one aggregate band at the top of the page. She is right, and this should be the page's centre of gravity, not the event list.

Partners & collaborations → /impact — yes, plus a thin strip on the homepage

Named organisations are trust transfer, and trust transfer works earliest. Full treatment on /impact with relationship type made explicit — employer, affiliate, campaign participant, host, coalition member. A condensed strip on the homepage. Governance: naming her employer, the Illinois Council on Problem Gambling, and the "Are You Really Winning?" campaign all need written clearance, and the campaign in particular must be worded as participation, never ownership.

Client journey → /book, not /impact — this is the disagreement

A journey diagram reduces friction at the moment of decision. On an events page it's decoration; on the booking page it's the thing that converts a hesitant programme chair. Recommendation: the canonical version lives on /book, with a three-step teaser at the foot of /impact linking through.

But there are two different journeys and she may mean the other one. Flag it to her rather than guessing:

  • The booking journey — enquiry → fit call → proposal → agreement → pre-event prep → delivery → follow-up. Belongs on /book.
  • The engagement journey — what she does around an event: needs assessment → session → participant materials → post-event outcome summary. This one belongs on /impact, and it is what makes her look like a partner rather than a hired speaker.

My read is she meant the second. It's a question for the questionnaire, not a call for us.

Elegant and impactful, now make it bold and brilliant.

She approved the look and then added two words. Those two words are a real brief, and the current build doesn't meet them yet — for reasons that are diagnosable rather than vague.

The diagnosis. The build achieves elegant through restraint: near-black ground, one accent, generous space, an editorial serif. But it achieves elegance by restraint, and bold and brilliant are the opposite instinct. Twelve consecutive near-black sections with one gold accent is a page that never raises its voice. Bold is not a brighter background — it's decisive contrast, larger type, saturated colour, and fewer elements each doing more. Keep the black. Change what happens on top of it.

Eight concrete moves

  1. Use her actual palette

    The site runs on one gold plus a four-stop headline gradient. Her brand direction specifies emerald, teal, royal blue, burnt orange, mustard, berry, coral, cream. The colours already exist as tiny icon chips in the formats grid — promote them from decoration to structure. Give each speaking track its own colour across its whole card and modal, not a 40px square.

  2. Break the black run — two full-colour sections

    Twelve dark sections in sequence produce fatigue and sameness. Two full-bleed colour sections fix it: emerald for Partnership, coral for Booking. The dark then reads as deliberate luxury rather than a default. This single change does more for "brilliant" than anything else on the list.

  3. Discipline the ribbon

    The gold wave currently runs behind every section at high opacity and crosses body copy, numerals and headlines. It is simultaneously the legibility problem and the reason every section looks identical. Three hero-level moments at full strength; 6–8% everywhere else; never behind body text.

  4. Widen the type scale

    Headlines around 64px against 18px body is a comfortable ratio, not a bold one. Push the two or three signature headlines significantly larger and let everything else get quieter. Bold comes from the gap between the loudest and quietest thing on the page.

  5. Keep the one-word-in-colour device — use it less

    "What she speaks about." / "Prevention that reaches people." / "Where she's making an impact." The device works. It is currently on every single section headline, which turns a signature into a template. Three uses, maximum.

  6. Real photography, graded consistently

    Nothing kills "brilliant" faster than images a viewer suspects are synthetic. Real photos of Tiffany, one consistent grade, at least two full-bleed. If the library is thin, a photo shoot is the highest-ROI line item in the whole project — it feeds the site, the media kit, and every social channel simultaneously.

  7. Reduce motion, orchestrate what's left

    One considered hero entrance and scroll reveals on section headers only. Scattered effects read as generated; one orchestrated moment reads as designed. Respect prefers-reduced-motion.

  8. Pick one signature

    The page needs one thing people remember. My candidate: the twenty-one topics as a colour-coded index — the only speaker site where you can see an entire body of expertise in one view, sorted by who it's for. That's her actual differentiator, made visual. Spend the boldness there and keep everything else disciplined.
