TL;DR & Key Findings

+

TL;DR

  • Reposition Tiffany Webb from "gambling prevention coordinator" (a job title) to a national Community Impact Strategist whose signature lane is culturally rooted gambling-harm prevention — arriving exactly as legalized sports betting turns problem gambling into a top-tier public-health story.
  • The umbrella personal brand should be structured as a Branded House: "Tiffany Webb" is the master brand; Speaking is the flagship revenue engine; GambleFreeGear ("Break the Silence") stays a distinct-but-endorsed sub-brand that visibly proves the mission rather than competing with it.
  • Realistic near-term speaker economics: launch at the emerging/regional tier (~$1,500–$5,000 per keynote; day-long workshops can reach $5,000–$10,000) with a credible 18–36 month path to the $5,000–$15,000 established-professional tier as a book, media features, video reel, and testimonials accrue.

Key Findings

01 Market timing is exceptional

Problem gambling has moved from niche to recognized public-health crisis on the back of legalized sports betting. This is genuine white space for a credentialed, community-rooted Black woman prevention leader with authentic storytelling.

02 Her differentiator is a rare triple-stack

Credentialed public-health expertise + frontline community-outreach volume (4,000+ hours) + authentic cultural storytelling ("Chicago soul, Louisiana heart"). None of her five reference brands occupy the gambling-prevention + health-equity + community-implementation corner.

03 Materials are strong but fragmented

Multiple decks, taglines, domains, and contact points create brand-consistency risk. The knowledge base harmonizes the voice and flags contradictions explicitly rather than silently resolving them.

04 The offer ladder IS the business model

Free content → low-cost resources → mid-tier workshops → high-value keynotes/consulting. Speaking is front and center; GambleFreeGear is the emotional and retail proof layer that markets the mission.

Structure

Brand Architecture

+

Recommended model: a Branded House with one endorsed sub-brand

David Aaker's framework distinguishes a Branded House (one master brand extends across offerings — Virgin, Google, FedEx) from a House of Brands (independent brands under a hidden parent — P&G, Unilever). For a person building authority, the Branded House is correct: the master brand is Tiffany Webb, and every venture should ladder up to and borrow credibility from her name. The single strategic exception is GambleFreeGear, which works best as an endorsed sub-brand — its own name, logo, and retail identity, but visibly vouched for ("GambleFreeGear, founded by Tiffany Webb").

  • Master brand: Tiffany Webb — Community Impact Strategist. Owns the reputation, story, voice, and audience.
  • Flagship pillar → Speaking (keynotes, panels, workshops): the primary revenue engine and authority driver.
  • Consulting / Program Design / Executive Advising: high-value, lower-volume; directly under her name.
  • Training Academy: productized workshops/curricula/certifications (mid-tier, scalable) — carries the "Learn. Change. Grow." signature.
  • Books & Signature Framework: authority assets that raise speaking fees.
  • Podcast: owned-media flywheel.
  • GambleFreeGear ("Break the Silence"): endorsed sub-brand — distinct retail/mission identity and handles, "by Tiffany Webb." The emotional and merchandise proof of the mission: cause apparel that acts as a "walking campaign" and conversation starter, reinforcing (not competing with) speaker authority.
  • "GambleFree Friends": community/peer-engagement extension of the GambleFreeGear mission (mentoring women in recovery, community cohorts). Keep under the GambleFreeGear halo.
  • "The Nonprofit Hustle": a distinct content/education line (likely podcast/book/course) for nonprofit-capacity and social-entrepreneur audiences. Endorsed sub-brand under her name; keep clearly separate from the gambling-prevention lane so the core positioning stays sharp.
Naming / relationship rule of thumb

Everything is "Tiffany Webb [offer]" EXCEPT the two mission/venture brands (GambleFreeGear, The Nonprofit Hustle), which carry their own names plus a visible "by Tiffany Webb" endorsement. This preserves focus while letting the ventures keep their own personality and audience — and insulates the master brand if any single venture underperforms.

Positioning & Competitive Landscape

+

Reference-brand teardown — what to borrow, what to avoid

Brené Brown — Research → framework → franchise

A University of Houston social-work researcher became a global authority via one viral idea (vulnerability), a repeatable framework (Dare to Lead), and a books/podcast engine (five #1 NYT bestsellers; Unlocking Us / Dare to Lead podcasts). Borrow: turn rigor into ONE memorable, teachable framework; pair academic credibility with relatable storytelling. Avoid: her celebrity-tier fee model and the "book years in advance" scale — not the near-term goal.

Simon Sinek — One idea, infinitely repeatable

"Start With Why" and the Golden Circle give him a purpose-first framework that anchors every talk (former ad strategist who shifted from brand work to leadership). Borrow: distill her philosophy ("prevention begins with a conversation") into a named model. Avoid: abstraction without implementation — her buyers reward practical takeaways.

Dan Pallotta — Contrarian manifesto for a sector

He reframed how society thinks about nonprofit overhead and executive pay; his authority rests on a bold, defensible point of view (TED talk, Uncharitable, Charity Case). Borrow: a signature POV (e.g., "prevention that ignores culture isn't prevention"), which also fuels "The Nonprofit Hustle." Avoid: provocation without warmth.

Cheryl "Action" Jackson (Minnie's Food Pantry) — Faith-driven, story-first social entrepreneur

A mother's-legacy origin story, a nickname ("Action"), 21M+ meals served, keynote + media (Good Morning America, HuffPost) + a Texas House resolution honoring her work. Borrow: the legacy/faith/"turn pain into power" narrative (which Tiffany already has) and the pursuit of official resolutions and media features as credibility markers. Avoid: tying the personal brand so tightly to one organization that it can't travel.

Kelly Fair (Polished Pebbles) — Chicago program-founder-turned-speaker

A signature niche (girls' mentoring/communication), two books, a memorable epithet ("The Mentor"), cross-sector logos (Google, ComEd, Verizon), and — critically — a booking contact separate from her nonprofit (an outside comms rep handles rates/availability). This is the closest structural comp to Tiffany's situation. Borrow: the founder-of-a-named-program → sought-after-speaker path; authored books as ladder rungs; separated booking mechanics.

White space Tiffany can own (that none of them occupy)

Culturally rooted gambling-harm prevention and community implementation. She sits at the intersection of (1) a fast-rising, under-served topic (gambling/sports-betting harm), (2) health equity and cultural fluency for communities prevention misses, and (3) practical, coalition-level implementation — not just inspiration. Her one-of-one lane:

"The prevention leader who makes the hardest conversations approachable — and hands communities a plan."

Why the topic is hot now (booking tailwinds)

Since the Supreme Court's 2018 Murphy v. NCAA decision, sports betting has expanded to ~39 operational states plus D.C. and Puerto Rico, and gambling is increasingly framed as a public-health crisis (Harvard/Lancet coverage; a bipartisan federal Gambling Disorder Health Study Act; university research). The NCPG Annual Conference (July 22–24, 2026, Nashville; Call for Presentations ran Nov 3–Dec 5, 2025), state councils (e.g., NY's "Champions of Change," Albany, March 10–12, 2026), Problem Gambling Awareness Month (March), and Gambling Disorder Screening Day (second Tuesday of March) form a ready-made CFP and stage ecosystem. Cultural relevance for Black and other communities of color is especially strong: research in Addiction and related literature reports higher problem-gambling prevalence among African Americans (~3.6%) and Hispanic Americans (~3.3%) than white Americans (~2.9%), and repeatedly calls for culturally specific prevention models — precisely Tiffany's edge.

Data anchors the studio can weave into talk descriptions

(Named sources — attribute precisely.)

National scale

The National Council on Problem Gambling (NCPG) estimates 9 million American adults suffer from a gambling problem, resulting in a national social cost of $14 billion annually (criminal-justice, healthcare, job-loss, and bankruptcy costs); NCPG's 2025 national survey found nearly 20 million U.S. adults — 8% — report indicators of a gambling problem. (Present these as different thresholds, not one number — see Caveats.)

Gambling & suicide (Track 2)

NCPG estimates "one in five people who have severe gambling problems will attempt suicide," about twice the rate of other addictions — best paired with the peer-reviewed meta-analysis cited by Wardle et al. (ScienceDirect, 2024): prevalence rates of 31% for suicidal ideation, 17% for suicide plans, and 16% for attempts among people with gambling disorders.

Gambling, DV & trauma (Track 3)

Dowling, Suomi, Jackson et al.'s systematic review and meta-analysis (Trauma, Violence, & Abuse, 2016) found over one-third of problem gamblers report being victims of physical intimate-partner violence (38.1%) or perpetrators (36.5%), and the prevalence of problem gambling among IPV perpetrators is 11.3%. (This is the original source behind the "37%" figure later cited by Harvard Magazine / Lancet.)

Sports betting & vulnerable young men

Two 2024 working papers — Hollenbeck & Larsen et al. (UCLA Anderson) and Baker, Balthrop, Johnson, Kotter & Pisciotta (NBER, "Gambling Away Stability") — found online sports-betting legalization increased household financial distress; one study reported bankruptcy rates rose ~28% and debt collections ~8% roughly two years post-legalization, with effects strongest among "the most economically vulnerable young men" (American Institute for Boys and Men). Public opinion is shifting too: per Pew Research Center, 43% of Americans now say legal sports betting is bad for society (up from 34% in 2022), and among men under 30 the share rose from 22% (2022) to 47% (2025).

2025 theme "Seeking Understanding"; 2026 theme "Caring Communities, Stronger Futures."
