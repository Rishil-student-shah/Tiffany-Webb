## ARTICLE 1 — full text

**Title:** What Gambling Prevention Actually Looks Like
**Category:** Prevention · **Read time:** 5 min
**Excerpt:** Most prevention campaigns are designed for people who are already looking. Here's what reaching everyone else requires.

> Most gambling prevention is built on a quiet assumption: that if we make good information available, the people who need it will come and find it.
>
> Fifteen years of doing this work in schools, clinics, and community centres has taught me that the assumption is backwards. The people at highest risk are almost always the least likely to seek anything out. Not because they don't care — because seeking help means naming a problem, and naming it means admitting it exists.
>
> So prevention that waits to be found mostly reaches people who were going to be fine anyway.
>
> **What actually works is less convenient.**
>
> It means showing up where people already are. Not an assembly the students tune out — a session in a space they chose to be in. Not a clinic pamphlet — a conversation in a church basement with someone the community already trusts vouching for you.
>
> It means partnering rather than arriving. Every community I've worked in already has people holding trust: pastors, coaches, barbers, recovery leaders, the woman who runs the food pantry. Prevention that goes through them lands. Prevention that arrives as an outside expert bounces.
>
> It means talking about gambling without making anyone the villain. The fastest way to end a prevention conversation is to make the person listening feel accused. Most people who develop gambling problems started with something completely ordinary — a bracket, an app their friends had, a scratch card someone gave them as a gift.
>
> **And it means accepting a slower measure of success.**
>
> Campaign metrics reward reach. Real prevention shows up as a father asking a question after everyone else has left the room. A school counsellor who now knows what to ask. A coalition that adds one screening question to an intake form.
>
> None of that photographs well. All of it works.
>
> If your organization is trying to reach a community that hasn't responded to anything yet, the problem usually isn't your message. It's the distance between where your message lives and where that community actually is. Close that distance, and the message takes care of itself.

## ARTICLE 2 — full text

**Title:** Don't Bet on Your Future: Why Youth Prevention Starts With a Conversation
**Category:** Youth Prevention · **Read time:** 5 min
**Excerpt:** Sports betting reached young people faster than prevention did. Here's how schools and parents can catch up.

> The gambling conversation with young people has changed completely, and most of the adults around them haven't caught up yet.
>
> When I started this work, gambling meant somewhere you had to go. Now it means an app, a group chat, and a game already on the screen in front of them. The distance between curiosity and a placed bet has collapsed to about four seconds.
>
> **The crossover is the part adults miss.**
>
> Long before anyone places a real bet, a lot of young people have spent years in environments built on gambling mechanics — loot boxes, in-game cases, odds dressed up as rewards. By the time an actual betting app arrives, the mechanics feel familiar. It doesn't register as gambling. It registers as a game they already know how to play.
>
> That's why "don't gamble" lands so poorly. In their experience, they aren't.
>
> **What works better is starting with how it's designed.**
>
> Young people are remarkably receptive when you stop moralizing and start explaining the machine. Why the app sends a notification at exactly that moment. Why the near-miss feels like almost-winning when it's just losing. Why the free bet isn't free. Nobody enjoys being told what to do; almost everyone enjoys being let in on how they're being played.
>
> That reframe moves the conversation from *you're doing something wrong* to *someone is doing something to you.* One closes a teenager down. The other opens them up.
>
> **The adults need their own session.**
>
> The most useful thing a school can do is run two conversations: one with students, one with the parents and staff around them. Adults consistently underestimate exposure — and often don't know that giving lottery tickets or scratch cards to minors is a genuine early-exposure pathway, not a harmless gift.
>
> Prevention here isn't about scaring anyone. It's about making sure that when a young person does have a question — about a friend, about a sibling, about themselves — there's an adult nearby who won't panic, won't lecture, and knows where to go next.
>
> That's it. That's the whole strategy. Make the conversation available before it's urgent.

## ARTICLE 3 — full text

**Title:** The Communities Prevention Reaches Last
**Category:** Health Equity · **Read time:** 4 min
**Excerpt:** Gambling harm doesn't fall evenly. Neither does prevention. That gap is a design problem, not an accident.

> Gambling harm does not fall evenly across communities. Research published in the journal *Addiction* has found higher problem-gambling prevalence among African Americans (3.6%) than among white Americans (2.9%).
>
> What gets discussed far less is the second gap: prevention resources don't fall evenly either. And unlike the first gap, that one is entirely a design choice.
>
> **Most prevention is built for a person who doesn't exist in every community.**
>
> The assumed audience has a stable schedule, transport, and a trusted relationship with a health system. They can attend a Tuesday-evening session. They'll follow a referral to an unfamiliar clinic. They'll read the pamphlet.
>
> Build every program around that person and you'll consistently reach some communities and consistently miss others — then conclude the missed communities were "hard to engage."
>
> They weren't hard to engage. They were engaged on terms that didn't fit.
>
> **Culturally rooted prevention starts from a different question.**
>
> Not *how do we deliver this program here?* but *who already holds trust here, and what would make this useful to them?*
>
> In practice that's slower and less scalable. It means going through the pastor, the coach, the recovery leader. It means the session happening after a service, or at a community fair, or in a barbershop. It means a referral that names a specific person, not just an organization.
>
> It also means acknowledging out loud that some communities have very good historical reasons to be cautious about health institutions arriving with advice.
>
> **The evidence base already asks for this.**
>
> The research literature has repeatedly called for culturally specific prevention models. What's usually missing isn't evidence — it's the willingness to spend the relationship time those models require.
>
> If your prevention numbers look strong in some parts of your service area and flat in others, that's worth reading as information rather than as a failure of the community. It usually means the program was built for one kind of person, and it's working exactly as designed.
