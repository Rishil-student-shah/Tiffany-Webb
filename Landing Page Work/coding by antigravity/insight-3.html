The Communities Prevention Reaches Last

Standard clinical programming assumes a level of trust that simply does not exist in historically overlooked communities.

In public health, we often design interventions in boardrooms and expect them to flourish on the streets. When they fail, the blame is placed on the community for being "hard to reach." But the community isn't hard to reach; our methodology is often tone-deaf to their reality.

Cultural Fluency in Practice

My Chicago roots and Louisiana heritage taught me early on that community is built on trust, not credentials. When addressing gambling harm in marginalized neighborhoods, you cannot lead with clinical assessments. You must lead with empathy and cultural fluency.

Cultural fluency means understanding that in some communities, discussing financial loss is taboo. It means knowing that the local barbershop or the church basement is a more effective venue for a screening event than a sterile clinic downtown.

"The community isn't hard to reach; our methodology is often tone-deaf to their reality."

Redesigning Outreach

To reach these communities, we must dismantle the clinical wall. We must partner with the gatekeepers of these neighborhoods—the faith leaders, the local coaches, the civic organizers. By equipping them with the right language and screening tools, we empower the community to heal itself from within.
